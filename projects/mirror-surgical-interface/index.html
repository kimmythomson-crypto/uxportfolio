mirrOR Surgical Interface Redesign

Client
Rods & Cones
Year
2021
Role
UX Design Lead
Focus
MedTech

mirrOR lets surgeons stream their point of view to remote experts mid-operation, but years of added features had left a toolbar packed with controls competing for attention at exactly the wrong moment. Six interviews across surgeons, support agents and product managers, plus an icon recognition test, showed users relied on just five or six of them. The redesign cut the bottom toolbar to six core controls and moved everything else into collapsible panels, without removing any functionality.

100%

of Harvard Medical School surgeons chose the new interface

The problem

A tool that had outgrown itself

Since launch, mirrOR had gained features one user request at a time, without anyone stepping back to look at the whole. The result was a toolbar packed with controls competing for attention at the moment surgeons most needed to focus: mid-operation, under pressure, with no margin for error.

The brief: simplify the interface without losing functionality.

Research

Listening to the people in the room

Six interviews across three groups: surgeons, the customer service agents who join live calls to troubleshoot, and product managers. All three said the same thing. The interface held far more controls than a live procedure ever needed, and users worked around features they never touched to reach the five or six they relied on.

An icon recognition test on the existing set, especially the smart glasses controls, showed which icons were understood at a glance and which caused hesitation.

6

The maximum number of icons on screen during surgery

In a high-pressure, high-stakes environment, visual noise isn’t just an inconvenience. It’s a risk. Every unnecessary element is a potential point of hesitation, at a moment when hesitation costs time.

The redesign

What changed and why

The bottom toolbar

Stripped back to the six controls needed during a live call: zoom, inclination, focus, exposure, contrast and pointer.

Secondary controls and cameras

Secondary controls moved to a collapsible side panel. Extra camera feeds became collapsible thumbnail strips, visible without pulling focus from the main view.

The interaction toolbox

A slide-in panel keeps collaboration and annotation tools on demand, leaving the main canvas clean.

Outcome

The interface surgeons chose

When the redesign rolled out, Rods & Cones kept the original interface available as a fallback, a deliberate choice to reduce risk and give users options.

Harvard Medical School surgeons unanimously chose the new one.
